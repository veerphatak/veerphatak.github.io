Seven weeks in the research and development team at TREND Networks, who design and manufacture test and measurement equipment for network infrastructure. I delivered three design projects, one of which was approved and released for manufacture.

Designing for a product rather than a brief

At university a design is finished when it meets the written specification. In a product team it is judged by whoever ends up using the equipment, often outdoors, in bad conditions and in a hurry. That changed what I treated as a finished design, and it is the main thing I took from the placement.

How the work ran

Every project followed the same loop. Model the change in SolidWorks, slice and print it the same day, assemble it against the real hardware, take it to whichever engineers had a stake in it, and use what came back to define the next version. Owning the office printing is what made that loop short enough to be useful: a version printed in the morning could be reviewed in the afternoon and superseded by the following day.

Across the three projects that came to sixteen versions in seven weeks. Each one existed because something specific had gone wrong in the version before it: a clearance that did not hold once the part was physical, a feature that was awkward to use, or a wall section that would not mould cleanly.

Battery compartment redesign

New EU regulations require batteries in products of this type to be replaceable by the user. This required reworking a component within a product already in production, inside a fixed internal envelope that could not be altered.

It had to protect the battery, survive handling on site, stay economical to manufacture, match the styling of the existing product, and be obvious enough that a user could change a battery without instructions. Those requirements pull against each other, and it took ten iterations before one was approved. That design was released for manufacture and is going into new units.

Instrument enclosure

My principal project was an enclosure for a handheld test instrument, developed from initial concept. The enclosure had to house a PCB that had already been manufactured, so the board was fixed and the design had to accommodate it: clearances, port positions and mounting points were all determined by existing hardware.

The first version was robust but large and angular. I reviewed it with the engineers in the team, took their feedback, and made the second version smaller and smoother. A further review produced a third version that refined the form again and added protection for the test connections. Three iterations, from a blank screen to a design the team was happy with.

Fibre spool

A spool that feeds fibre during a test and protects it in storage. The constraint was volume: it had to hold as much fibre as possible in the space available inside each tester case. Three iterations, most of the work going into the winding and routing arrangement needed to maximise stored length without damaging the fibre.

3D printing

I was responsible for all 3D printing within the office. Every iteration of my own designs was printed and assessed the same day, along with components required by other engineers for product testing, including speaker rests and button holders.

Printing my own models found problems that were invisible on screen: warping, wall sections thinner in practice than they looked in CAD, and tolerances too tight to assemble. Checking each printed part against the model improved my tolerancing and my judgement of what would actually manufacture.

What carried over

Designing around hardware I do not control is the same problem as a wind tunnel model that has to mount in an existing working section, and I am using that directly as CAD and manufacturing lead on my final-year wind tunnel model.

Printing every version also taught me that a part is not verified until it exists. That is the same argument as checking a structural prediction against a loaded wing on the UAV project, or checking a lap time model against telemetry on the F1 model.

Relevant skills

  • Designing to fixed external constraints, including existing hardware, a sealed envelope and regulatory requirements.
  • Designing for the end user and for the manufacturing process, including injection moulding.
  • Incorporating engineering feedback across multiple design iterations.
  • Delivering work that passed internal review and entered production.
