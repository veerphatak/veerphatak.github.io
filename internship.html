R&D Engineering Internship, TREND Networks

Seven weeks in the research and development team at TREND Networks, who design and manufacture test and measurement equipment for network infrastructure. Three design projects, and one of them is now being manufactured.

A note on detail. TREND Networks has asked that specific products are not identified publicly, so this page describes the engineering rather than naming the hardware. Full detail is available on request.

The difference between university design and product design

At university a design is finished when it satisfies the brief you were marked against. In an R&D team the brief is a person: someone who will hold the product in the field, in poor light, possibly in a hurry, and who will never think about whoever designed it. That changes what "finished" means, and it was the most useful thing I took from the seven weeks.

Battery compartment redesign

New EU rules require batteries in products like these to be replaceable by the user. That meant reworking a part inside a product already on sale, and the space it had to fit into was fixed and not negotiable.

The design had to protect the battery, survive being handled on site, stay sensible to manufacture, look like it belonged on the rest of the product rather than bolted onto it, and be obvious enough that someone could change a battery without being told how. Those requirements pull against each other, which is why it took ten versions before one was approved. That design was released for manufacture and is going into new units.

Instrument enclosure

My main project was an enclosure for a handheld test instrument, taken from a blank start. It had to house a PCB that had already been manufactured, so the board was fixed and everything else had to work around it: clearances, port positions and mounting points all had to fit what already existed.

The first version was rugged, but large, angular and clunky. I took it to the engineers around me, they told me what was not working, and the second came out smaller and smoother. Another round of feedback produced a third version that refined the shape further and added protection for the test connections. Three iterations, each one driven by somebody telling me what was wrong with the last.

Fibre spool

A spool to feed fibre out during a test and protect it the rest of the time. The governing constraint here was volume: fitting as much fibre as possible into the space available inside each tester case. Three versions, most of the thinking going into how tightly the fibre could be wound and routed without damaging it.

Owning the 3D printing

I ran all the 3D printing for the office. Every iteration of mine went from CAD to a part in my hand the same day, and so did parts other engineers needed for product testing, things like speaker rests and button holders.

Printing your own designs teaches you what a screen will not: where a part warps, where a wall is thinner than it looked on the model, and where a tolerance you picked is too tight to actually assemble. Comparing the printed part against the CAD each time made me considerably more sceptical of my own models, which I consider an improvement.

What this demonstrates

  • Designing to fixed, real constraints: an existing PCB, a sealed envelope, a regulation.
  • Designing for the person who uses the product, and for the process that manufactures it, including injection moulding.
  • Taking engineering feedback repeatedly and using it, rather than defending the first idea.
  • Delivering work that passed review and went into production.
