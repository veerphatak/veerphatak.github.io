R&D Engineering Internship, TREND Networks

Seven weeks in the research and development team at TREND Networks, who design and manufacture test and measurement equipment for network infrastructure. I delivered three design projects, one of which was approved and released for manufacture.

Designing for a product rather than a brief

University design work is assessed against a written specification, and a design is complete once that specification is satisfied. In a product development team the design is ultimately judged by the person using the equipment, often in the field, in poor conditions and under time pressure. That distinction changes what constitutes a finished design, and it was the most significant thing I took from the placement.

Battery compartment redesign

New EU regulations require batteries in products of this type to be replaceable by the user. This required reworking a component within a product already in production, inside a fixed internal envelope that could not be altered.

The design had to protect the battery, withstand handling on site, remain economical to manufacture, match the styling of the existing product, and be sufficiently intuitive that a user could change a battery without instruction. These requirements conflict with one another, and reconciling them took ten design iterations before one was approved. That design was released for manufacture and is being fitted to new units.

Instrument enclosure

My principal project was an enclosure for a handheld test instrument, developed from initial concept. The enclosure had to house a PCB that had already been manufactured, so the board was fixed and the design had to accommodate it: clearances, port positions and mounting points were all determined by existing hardware.

The first version was robust but large and angular. I reviewed it with the engineers in the team, incorporated their feedback, and the second version was smaller and more refined. A further review produced a third version that improved the form again and added protection for the test connections. Each iteration was driven by specific technical feedback on the previous one.

Fibre spool

A spool to feed fibre during testing and to protect it in storage. The governing constraint was volume: the design had to accommodate as much fibre as possible within the space available inside each tester case. This took three iterations, the majority of the work being the winding and routing arrangement required to maximise stored length without damaging the fibre.

3D printing

I was responsible for all 3D printing within the office. Every iteration of my own designs was printed and assessed the same day, along with components required by other engineers for product testing, including speaker rests and button holders.

Producing physical parts from my own models identified issues that were not apparent on screen: warping, wall sections thinner in practice than they appeared in CAD, and tolerances too tight to assemble. Comparing each printed part against the model improved both my tolerancing and my judgement of manufacturability.

Relevant skills

  • Designing to fixed external constraints, including existing hardware, a sealed envelope and regulatory requirements.
  • Designing for the end user and for the manufacturing process, including injection moulding.
  • Incorporating engineering feedback across multiple design iterations.
  • Delivering work that passed internal review and entered production.
