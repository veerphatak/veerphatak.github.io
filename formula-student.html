Seasons in the team

The job

A student team gets very little track time, and has limited tyres and a limited budget. Every run spends all three. My job in race engineering was to make sure that when the car ran, it ran a setup we could justify, and that we already knew what we were trying to learn from the session.

Simulation

I built lap time and tyre load and degradation models in MATLAB and Excel, and used them to rank setup changes by how much lap time each one was worth. Sensitivity studies were run before a test rather than after it, so we arrived with a shortlist that had already been filtered instead of a list of guesses.

That mattered because the expensive failure for a student team is spending a session establishing that a change did nothing.

Data analysis and correlation

I analysed race and qualifying laps in MoTeC i2, comparing logged channel data against the simulation output and against the driver's description of the car. The three sources disagreed regularly. A model that matched the logged data while contradicting the driver was usually missing a physical effect, and tracking down which one was where most of the work sat.

Predicting, measuring and then reconciling the two is the part of the role I would want to carry into a professional performance or strategy group.

Chassis

Alongside the race engineering work I researched integrating the front roll hoop into the chassis for future cars, and presented the technical and feasibility findings to the department and to team management.

Results

During my time with the team SUFST finished 2nd in the FSUK Simulator Championship and lap time challenge in 2025, and 10th overall at FSUK 2025. The simulator result is the one most directly connected to the setup and simulation work.

A representative lap time model

The team's model is not mine to publish, so I wrote an equivalent one from scratch for this site. It is a quasi-steady lap time simulation with tyre degradation, on a synthetic track with generic vehicle parameters. The method is the one I used in the team, and the figures below are its output.

Method

The solver makes three passes over a discretised lap. It first finds the cornering limit at every point. That has to be solved rather than assumed, because grip depends on downforce, downforce depends on speed, and speed is the quantity being solved for:

function v = cornerLimitSpeed(car, radius, mu)
% Speed at which the car is exactly on the lateral grip limit.
k       = mu * 0.5 * car.rho * car.cl_a / car.mass;   % aero grip term
bracket = 1.0 ./ radius - k;
v2      = inf(size(radius));
ok      = bracket > 0;
v2(ok)  = mu * 9.81 ./ bracket(ok);
v       = min(sqrt(v2), car.v_max);
end

Where that bracket falls to zero or below, the car is generating downforce faster than the corner demands it and is no longer grip limited there, so power and gearing set the speed instead. A forward pass then applies acceleration and a backward pass applies braking, both sharing tyre capacity with cornering through a friction ellipse. Lap time is the integral of ds/v.

Tyre degradation

Grip falls as the tyres do work, so the model reduces peak friction through a stint, and reduces it faster when the car loads the tyres harder.

Setup sensitivity

Sweeping downforce shows the main trade-off. More downforce raises cornering speed and raises drag, and since most of the added drag is induced it grows with the square of the downforce. More downforce also works the tyres harder, which raises the degradation rate across a stint.

Those two results are why the model was worth building. A figure of 0.130 s per 10 kg turns a general preference for a lighter car into a target a design decision can be held against, and the gap between the qualifying and race optima is not something a team settles reliably by feel.
