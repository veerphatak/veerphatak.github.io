Fixed-Wing UAV Wing Design

Second-year group design project, ranked 1st in the cohort. I was the aerodynamics and stability lead and designed the wing structure, and the wing achieved the largest operating speed range of any team in the year group.

The brief

We had to design the wing for a fixed-wing UAV, marked mainly on the range between its minimum and maximum operating speed, within fixed limits on mass and volume. That needs enough lift at low speed to stay airborne and low enough drag at high speed to keep accelerating. Push either end too hard and you lose the other, or you run out of mass budget.

Structural design of the wing

A wide speed range is only worth having if the wing holds its shape while producing it. I worked on the spar and rib structure, where the requirement was the most stiffness per unit mass we could get in bending and in torsion.

I used a rectangular spar section rather than an I-beam, because it was easier to manufacture and to integrate into the ribs. The simpler section allowed long uninterrupted runs with the wood grain oriented along the load, and the stiffness gained that way outweighed what a more complex section would have given back at the joints.

I developed the structure on a bending and torsion rig I built, testing between iterations:

  • The initial single spar gave a second moment of area of 13,643 mm4.
  • After the first structural test, a second spar was added further aft in the ribs, contributing 11,539 mm4.
  • After the second test, cross members were added between the two spars, using the spars as webs to form a box spar. This raised the second moment of area and the enclosed volume substantially, the latter being what governs torsional stiffness.

The crosses were there to save weight without losing torsional strength. Both predictions came out conservative against test:

  • Bending: predicted tip deflection of 18.7 mm against a measured 13.8 mm.
  • Torsion: predicted twist of 1.57° against a measured 0.42°.

The rib cutout pattern came from a NACA study of wooden rib patterns and specific strength. I carried the same truss logic into the spars and tapered their second moment of area along the span to follow the lift distribution, so the wing was not carrying material out to the tip that the tip did not need.

Aerodynamic design

I led the aerodynamic configuration work. The aerofoil was selected in XFOIL with forced transition at 0.16c, screening sections above 12% thickness so the structure had depth to work with, and comparing maximum lift coefficient, maximum lift-to-drag ratio and the angle of attack separating the two. N22 and NACA 2412 came out ahead, and NACA 2412 was selected after further analysis of minimum speed in XFLR5.

We chose the planform taper with a ranking matrix backed by XFLR5 sweeps, converting the aerodynamic data into a predicted speed range for each candidate layout. Setting the criteria before looking at the options meant the choice could be defended afterwards on the numbers.

Speed range at the low end was extended with single-slot Fowler flaps at 30% chord, deployed to 45° with a 0.02c overlap and a 0.04c slot, giving a lift coefficient multiplier of 1.87 and around a 20% increase in area. Counter-rotating rectangular vortex generators were added to delay stall. In flight the wing showed strong roll authority with no tip stall at high angle of attack.

Result

The XFLR5 and Python analysis predicted a 23.1 m/s speed range for the chosen layout. Flight testing measured 19.8 m/s between minimum and maximum airspeed, the largest speed range of all participating groups, and the project was ranked 1st in the cohort.

The aircraft came in 3.3 m/s short of the predicted range. The aerodynamic model was optimistic, most likely on high-lift device performance and on the drag of a real assembled airframe against a clean analysis case. The structural model erred the other way and under-predicted stiffness in both bending and torsion, which says it was not crediting the box spar and cross members with as much as they were doing.

Project management

I produced the Gantt charts and resource plans used to run the project. The work split into research, design, manufacture, integration and testing, with the critical path tracked and buffer time placed ahead of the high-risk tasks. We finished ahead of schedule, which left time to check the analysis properly before submitting it.

Relevant skills

  • Structural design for stiffness per unit mass, validated against physical test.
  • Iterating a design on test results rather than committing to the first configuration.
  • Choosing between options against criteria set and documented in advance.
  • Planning and delivering a group project against a critical path.
