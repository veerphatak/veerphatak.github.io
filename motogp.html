Cornering Aerodynamics of a MotoGP-Style Motorcycle

Final-year group design project at the University of Southampton, combining CFD with wind tunnel testing. I am the team leader, the aerodynamic design lead, and the lead for CAD development and manufacture of the test model.

CFD Wind Tunnel CAD Manufacture Team Leadership In Progress
Project status: started September 2026 and running through to spring 2027. This page describes the scope and my responsibilities. Results will be added once the work has actually produced them.

The problem

Most motorcycle aerodynamic development is done with the bike upright and pointing straight ahead, because that is the easy case to model and the easy case to test. Racing motorcycles spend a large share of a lap leaned over. Once the machine rolls into a corner the flow field is no longer symmetric, the frontal area presented to the air changes, and the aerodynamic forces and moments acting on the bike change with it.

That makes cornering a harder and more interesting problem than straight-line running, and it is the case where aerodynamic behaviour matters most to a rider. This project investigates that regime using both computational and experimental methods, so the two can be checked against each other rather than trusted on their own.

My role

  • Team leader. Coordinating the group, setting the plan of work, and liaising with our supervisors, Dr David Marshall and Dr Renan Soares.
  • Aerodynamic design lead. Responsible for the aerodynamic direction of the project across both the CFD and the wind tunnel programme.
  • CAD development and manufacturing lead. Responsible for the CAD model and for getting the wind tunnel test model physically made.

Approach

The project runs two strands that have to agree with each other. CFD gives full-field information and the freedom to sweep conditions cheaply. Wind tunnel testing gives real measurements on a real model, with all the awkwardness that entails. The value is in the comparison: where the two disagree, something is wrong with the model, the mesh, the setup or the assumptions, and finding out which is the actual engineering work.

Building the test model myself is deliberate. A geometry that is straightforward to draw is not always straightforward to manufacture, mount, or instrument in a tunnel, and being responsible for both the CAD and the manufacture keeps those constraints in view from the start rather than discovering them late.

What this demonstrates

  • Leading a technical team on an open-ended problem with no predetermined answer.
  • Working across the full aerodynamic development loop: geometry, CFD, physical model, tunnel testing, and correlation between prediction and measurement.
  • Designing for manufacture and test, not just for analysis.
