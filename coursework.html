Karman-Trefftz Aerofoil Analysis

Advanced Aeronautics (SESA3025) · Graded 74%

This coursework generates an aerofoil by conformal mapping, analyses it with a panel method, and then compares it against a real NACA section in a viscous solver. My assigned parameters were a thickness factor of ε = 0.07, camber β = 0.07 and trailing-edge angle τ = 0.05.

Geometry generation

A Karman-Trefftz aerofoil is produced by mapping a circle in the complex plane onto an aerofoil shape, with three parameters controlling thickness, camber and trailing-edge angle. I swept each parameter independently to show how the geometry responds, and extracted maximum thickness and camber, with their chordwise positions, directly from the resulting coordinates.

Converting those geometric values to the nearest standard designation identified the NACA 3509 as the closest equivalent section, which became the benchmark for comparison. The two share a global thickness distribution but differ at the leading and trailing edges, which is a direct consequence of how the conformal mapping closes the trailing edge.

Panel method and viscous comparison

I implemented a doublet panel method in Python to estimate lift, and used it to run an order-of-accuracy study against panel count, plus a comparison of cosine against uniform panel spacing. Cosine spacing clusters panels where the geometry changes fastest, which is why it converges faster for the same number of panels.

The aerofoils were then compared in XFOIL across angle of attack, Reynolds number and transition criterion, looking at the boundary layer rather than just the force coefficients.

Minimum-Length Supersonic Nozzle by the Method of Characteristics

Aerothermodynamics (SESA3029)

The method of characteristics is a way of solving supersonic flow by following the lines along which information actually travels. This coursework applies it to design the shortest nozzle that will accelerate a flow to a target Mach number without generating shocks.

My assigned case was M_des = 2.3 in helium, so γ = 1.667 rather than the familiar 1.4, which changes the Prandtl-Meyer function and therefore every downstream value.

The design starts at the expansion corner, where the wall turns outwards to expand the flow smoothly. I used five characteristic lines, each representing an expansion wave reflecting between the centreline and the wall. At every intersection the flow angle θ and Prandtl-Meyer angle ν are found from the Riemann invariants of the two incoming characteristics, which gives the local Mach number and Mach angle. Tracing those lines outward gives the wall coordinates, and hence the minimum nozzle length.

The interesting part is why it is the minimum length: any shorter and the expansion cannot be cancelled cleanly, so waves reflect off the wall and coalesce into shocks, which is precisely what the method is designed to avoid.

Wind Tunnel Testing of a NACA 0020 Aerofoil

Aerodynamics laboratories

Three linked wind tunnel experiments on a symmetric NACA 0020 section, written up as a single report: surface pressure distribution against angle of attack, boundary layer development towards the trailing edge, and lift and drag on a finite half-wing.

The value of the exercise is the comparison. The measured results were checked against thin aerofoil theory, against XFOIL and XFLR5, and against CFD, and the report is largely about explaining the discrepancies rather than hiding them: viscous effects the inviscid theory cannot capture, laminar to turbulent transition along the boundary layer, and the tip effects and induced drag that appear once the wing is finite rather than two-dimensional.

Working out which of four sources to trust when they disagree, and why, is the part of experimental aerodynamics that actually transfers to a wind tunnel programme.

Finite Element Analysis of a Wing Box

Digital Aerospace (SESA3042)

Structural analysis of an aircraft wing box in ANSYS Mechanical: spars, ribs, skin and stringers, loaded for both a positive-g (wing up) and negative-g (wing down) manoeuvre, with the requirement that no component yields.

Most of the engineering here is in the mesh rather than the result. I ran a convergence study from roughly 24,000 to 1.9 million elements, tracking peak stress against solve time and memory. Peak stress changed by well under a percent across that range while cost rose by orders of magnitude, which is the whole argument for convergence testing: past a certain point you are buying precision you cannot use, and the honest answer is to stop refining and say why.
