Tracking Plastics in Moving Water

An individual Master's research project developing a Python and OpenCV measurement system that converts high-speed footage of plastic particles in a laboratory flume into validated trajectory and velocity data.

Background

The transport of plastic debris in rivers and oceans depends strongly on the shape, size and orientation of each fragment. A sphere, a rod and a flat disc of the same material behave differently in the same flow, and quantifying that behaviour is a prerequisite for modelling where plastics accumulate and how they might be intercepted.

The experimental difficulty is that video footage of a particle is not in itself measurement data. Quantitative analysis requires the position of the particle in every frame, determined reliably in the presence of reflections, bubbles, varying illumination and the flume walls, and then converted from image coordinates into physical velocities. Developing that conversion, from raw footage to validated trajectory data, was the core of the project.

Scope of the work

I developed a modular Python and OpenCV pipeline which:

  • Detects and tracks a particle through every frame of high-speed video, using a separate tracker tuned for each particle geometry (sphere, rod, disc).
  • Smooths and validates each trajectory using Kalman filtering and automated quality control checks designed to identify incorrect detections.
  • Calibrates image coordinates into physical distances using a per-view homography.
  • Derives the physics: instantaneous and streamwise velocity, orientation, projected area, and pooled comparisons between particle types, with every report figure regenerated directly from the exported data.

Technical approach

Geometry-specific tracking

A single generic tracker is not sufficient, as a tumbling rod, a rotating disc and a rolling sphere present substantially different visual signatures. The pipeline therefore uses three purpose-built trackers:

  • Spheres: colour and greyscale segmentation, including discrimination between red and white spheres, followed by Kalman smoothing.
  • Rods: binarised contour extraction with a quadrilateral fitted to the rod in each frame, orientation estimation, and Kalman smoothing.
  • Discs: binarised contours with projected-area and orientation extraction, capturing the change in presented face as the disc rotates.

Maintaining track

Acquiring the particle initially is considerably easier than retaining it for the duration of a run. Two specific failure modes were addressed in the design:

  • Automatic initialisation by motion. Instead of manually marking the starting position, the pipeline uses frame differencing and shape descriptor scoring (circularity and solidity) to identify the frame in which the particle enters the field of view, and acquires it automatically.
  • Drift prevention. A normalised cross-correlation template, updated gradually so that it accommodates changing illumination while resisting sudden changes, keeps the tracker centred on the particle. Without it the tracker tends to migrate onto a high-contrast feature such as a container corner, a common and easily missed failure.

Kalman smoothing and calibration

Frame-by-frame detections contain noise. A Kalman filter smooths each trajectory into a physically plausible path, filling small gaps and suppressing single-frame jitter while preserving genuine motion. This is necessary because velocity is obtained by differentiation, which amplifies positional noise. Image positions are then mapped to physical coordinates using a per-view homography, with explicit verification that the calibration is appropriate for each camera view.

Implementation

The pipeline comprises approximately 9,400 lines of Python across 23 scripts: three trackers, batch runners for each experiment, the physics and plotting scripts, and the validation tools. Four elements account for most of the functionality.

1. Isolating the particle

Each frame is blurred, background subtracted using an averaged background constructed from frames preceding the particle's entry, and then thresholded. The threshold uses Otsu's method, which selects the threshold automatically from the image histogram, with a lower bound applied so that a frame containing no particle cannot produce an invalid threshold and saturate the image with noise:

def otsu_threshold_with_floor(gray_image, min_threshold):
    otsu_thr, bw = cv2.threshold(gray_image, 0, 255,
                                 cv2.THRESH_BINARY + cv2.THRESH_OTSU)
    if otsu_thr < min_threshold:
        _, bw = cv2.threshold(gray_image, int(round(min_threshold)),
                              255, cv2.THRESH_BINARY)

Red spheres are handled separately, segmented in HSV colour space using cv2.inRange, as hue provides considerably better separation from the background than intensity.

2. Selecting the correct contour

Thresholding generally leaves several candidate regions, such as a reflection, a bubble or a shadow on the flume wall. Each contour is scored on shape using the same metrics across all three trackers:

circularity = 4.0 * np.pi * area / (perimeter * perimeter)
solidity    = area / hull_area          # area vs its own convex hull
aspect_ratio = max(w, h) / min(w, h)

Circularity is 1.0 for a perfect circle and decreases for irregular shapes, which distinguishes a sphere from elongated glare. Solidity compares a region against its own convex hull and identifies contours with concave sections, typically caused by two objects merging. Aspect ratio performs the equivalent function for rods, where the expected shape is long and narrow.

3. Kalman filtering

Detections vary by one or two pixels between frames, and because velocity is a derivative this variation is amplified rather than averaged out. A Kalman filter estimates position and velocity together, predicting the expected position of the particle and correcting that prediction using the measurement:

kf = cv2.KalmanFilter(4, 2)          # state: x, y, vx, vy | measured: x, y
kf.transitionMatrix = np.array([[1, 0, 1, 0],
                                [0, 1, 0, 1],
                                [0, 0, 1, 0],
                                [0, 0, 0, 1]], dtype=np.float32)
kf.processNoiseCov     = np.diag([0.001, 0.001, 0.008, 0.008]).astype(np.float32)
kf.measurementNoiseCov = np.diag([1.2, 1.2]).astype(np.float32)

The transition matrix implements a constant velocity model, in which the next position equals the current position plus the current velocity. The two noise covariance matrices determine the relative weighting between the model prediction and the measurement. Measurement noise is set substantially above process noise, indicating that the detections are less reliable than the motion model, which produces heavier smoothing. Excessive smoothing would cause the filter to generate motion the particle did not exhibit, introducing systematic error into the velocity results.

4. Conversion to physical units

All of the preceding stages operate in image coordinates. A homography, computed from a calibration target within the scene, maps those coordinates onto the physical plane of the flume and corrects for the camera not being normal to it. Velocities in mm/s, comparable between runs, are obtained only after this transformation.

Quality control

Validation was treated as a required stage of the process. For every processed run the workflow checked:

  • that the particle was detected continuously through the reported section, with no undetected gaps;
  • annotated videos and still overlays against the original footage, confirming that the trajectory followed the particle rather than a reflection or bubble;
  • that the calibration and homography were appropriate for the camera view;
  • that velocity plots contained no artefacts arising from missed detections;
  • agreement between repeated trials before any particle-type trend was pooled.

Results and analysis

From the validated trajectories the pipeline computed instantaneous speed and streamwise velocity against time for each run, velocity against normalised projected area for rods and discs, rod orientation against velocity, and a pooled comparison of velocity behaviour across spheres, rods and discs. Every figure was regenerated directly from the exported CSV files, so that any plotted value could be traced back to its source data.
